# Similarity is a question about an operation, not just an angle

Date: 2026-09-22. This is a methods assessment, not a decipherment or a new empirical result. The user's proposed token-cosine comparison is useful as a **diagnostic**, but raw nearest neighbours cannot establish a vocabulary's semantics or the route by which the model answers. The immediate priority is to test the causal route in the original model; any similarity study should be judged against that behavior and against form/context controls.

## What the vector denotes

Three objects that are often called “token embeddings” answer different questions: (1) the model's learned input row for a tokenizer ID, before context; (2) a hidden state at a particular position and layer, after context; and (3) a token-linked J-lens direction obtained by transporting an output word readout backward through an averaged Jacobian. Qwen's rows are about Qwen's English/subword vocabulary. Voynich EVA groups in a separately trained model have different coordinates and no validated meanings. Comparing vectors *across* those models by raw cosine is undefined without an independently tested alignment. Multi-token words also have no unique input row.

For vectors \(x,y\), Euclidean cosine is \(x^Ty/(\|x\|\|y\|)\). It is invariant to shared orthogonal rotations and positive scaling, but **not** to a general invertible change of representation basis. If \(x'=Ax\), then its numerator becomes \(x^TA^TAy\); different features can dominate merely through a coordinate stretch. A decoder can be transformed inversely and preserve the model's input-output function while these cosines change. Thus a cosine neighborhood is not intrinsically a functional or semantic neighborhood. This is a mathematical deduction, not a reported empirical result. [Park, Choe & Veitch](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03658) formalize why the inner product matters for linear concepts; [Kornblith et al.](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v97/kornblith19a.html) compare invariant representation metrics and show limitations of overly general invariance in high dimension.

A concrete counterexample makes the ambiguity sharp. Let \(x=(1,1)\) and \(y=(1,-1)\): their cosine is zero. Stretch the first coordinate by 10, so \(A=\mathrm{diag}(10,1)\). Their transformed cosine becomes \(99/101\approx0.9802\). Stretch the second coordinate instead and the cosine becomes \(-99/101\). For a downstream linear readout \(z=Wh\), choose \(W'=WA^{-1}\); then \(W'Ah=Wh\) for every input, despite the radically different angles. This does not mean the trained Qwen network actually uses an arbitrary coordinate gauge; it means an angle alone lacks the invariance needed to certify a functional statement.

For a fixed prompt and output target, a more relevant local object is the Jacobian \(J=\partial z/\partial h\). Two perturbations can be compared by the effect vectors \(J\delta_1,J\delta_2\), or by a task-specific quadratic form \(\delta^TJ^TWJ\delta\), where \(W\) weights measured output changes. For next-token distributions, the local KL metric is \(J^T(\mathrm{diag}(p)-pp^T)J\) when \(J\) maps hidden states to logits. This metric depends on prompt and layer, can be rank deficient, and its local approximation need not predict a large intervention. The relevant empirical test is therefore whether a geometry measure predicts held-out **causal effects** better than raw cosine and nuisance baselines. Causal interchange interventions supply a stronger criterion for an aligned variable than decoding alone [Geiger et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02997).

There is a useful invariance test for this proposed metric. Under the same coordinate change \(h'=Ah\), the logit Jacobian is \(J'=JA^{-1}\) and an intervention is \(\delta'=A\delta\). The pullback Fisher quadratic form satisfies \(\delta'^TJ'^T(\mathrm{diag}(p)-pp^T)J'\delta'=\delta^TJ^T(\mathrm{diag}(p)-pp^T)J\delta\). Thus it compares the *behavioral effect* of edits without depending on an invertible hidden-coordinate stretch. The Fisher form is positive semidefinite rather than a guaranteed full metric: directions invisible to the selected output distribution have zero length. With a small answer vocabulary, its rank can be tiny. A regularized inverse covariance or Mahalanobis distance has the opposite issue: numerical choices about shrinkage and low-variance axes can dominate. Neither should be declared a universal “true semantic distance.”

The first-order Jacobian also assumes \(z(h+\delta)\approx z(h)+J\delta\). PATH-0002 edits are whole donor residuals, not necessarily infinitesimal; nonlinear gating, attention reweighting and generated-token feedback can violate that approximation. A sound analysis therefore compares three levels: local derivatives, exact first-token distribution changes after the edit, and complete generated-answer changes. The exact distribution can be compared by Jensen–Shannon divergence or by a task-specific logit margin; the complete answer must be scored under the frozen rule. Those measures answer different questions. PATH-0001 already showed why a large donor-margin gain is not interchangeable with a full correct answer.

Contextual vectors are anisotropic and shift with layer and context [Ethayarajh](https://aclanthology.org/D19-1006/); frequency can distort cosine-based similarity [Zhou et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.05092). Centering/whitening or CKA/RSA can help compare *populations* of activations, but whitening changes the metric and CKA/RSA remain correlational. A high-capacity probe can learn arbitrary labels; random-label controls and capacity matter [Hewitt & Liang](https://aclanthology.org/D19-1275/). Similarity analyses of Voynich text already exist, including [Montemurro & Zanette's co-occurrence analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3689824/) and [Timm & Schinner's token-similarity generating account](https://doi.org/10.1080/01611194.2019.1596999). Their structural observations do not provide word meanings. The task here is to test additional discriminatory power, not to redescribe clusters.

A particularly direct predecessor is [Schurger-Foy, Rossi & Tkatch's EPFL course project on Voynich word embeddings](https://www.epfl.ch/labs/mlo/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/crpmlcourse-paper1311.pdf). They trained fastText variants on a roughly comparable-size Latin reference and a Voynich transcription, used artificial nonmorpheme strings and random word sets as controls, and compared cosine-distance clustering of prefix/suffix families. They explicitly warned that two-dimensional t-SNE clusters were unreliable. Their paper reports sensitivity to hyperparameters and disagreement with earlier suffix assignments (including `-ody`). This is useful methodological precedent, **not** a peer-reviewed decipherment or independent semantic validation. Its fastText construction includes character n-grams by design, so spelling overlap can itself drive a neighborhood; their artificial-string control is a good start but not a substitute for a structured copy/mutate null or held-out historical anchor. We should use their reported disagreement as a reason to freeze transcription and morphology claims before testing, not as evidence for one suffix interpretation.

The earlier [Voynich2Vec project by Baum and Merrill](https://lambdaviking.com/blog/2018/voynich2vec/) is even closer to the user's suggestion. Its authors report fastText-based suffix neighborhoods and preliminary section clustering, while saying their unsupervised cross-language alignment to Latin was noise on this small corpus. Their own discussion flags transcription disagreement and data sparsity. These are author-reported exploratory observations, not reproduced project results. Taken together with the EPFL disagreement, they motivate a metric comparison with explicit null generators and frozen held-out tests; rerunning nearest-neighbor cosine alone would repeat prior work without resolving its central ambiguity.

Mean subtraction is not a free fix: it removes directions shared across the chosen sample, and the chosen sample can be dominated by one section, prompt template or frequency band. Whitening magnifies low-variance axes unless regularized, so an apparent new cluster could reflect noise. Linear CKA compares centered sample Gram matrices and is invariant to orthogonal rotation and isotropic scaling, which is useful for comparing **whole representations** across layers, but it does not tell us which token pair is semantically related or which computation uses that relation. RSA likewise compares patterns of pairwise dissimilarities under a declared distance and sample population; changing either can change the answer. There is no contradiction if a CKA analysis finds layer correspondence while an individual token-cosine plot is unstable.

Vocabulary meaning is also unidentifiable from text alone under many plausible generators. If a hidden lexicon maps abstract labels to symbols, a permutation of those labels with a matching inverse permutation in the generator yields exactly the same observed corpus. Contextual statistics can constrain **relations** between symbol types, but cannot name an unlabeled concept without an external anchor or asymmetry that breaks that equivalence. A manuscript word cluster resembling the distribution of plant names is an interesting hypothesis, not a named translation. This is an identifiability argument, not an assertion that the Voynich manuscript uses such a model.

## A viable future vocabulary-structure test

Use only a frozen manuscript-training split and a fresh held-out **folio** split; keep transcription variants and glyph/space uncertainty explicit. Define objects separately for EVA glyphs, physical groups, model subword tokens, and contextual occurrences. Compare raw cosine, centered cosine, regularized covariance/Mahalanobis distance, context-neighborhood overlap, and downstream-effect similarity. Avoid selecting a metric by a visual map. Predeclare an outcome such as retrieval of held-out context distributions or known synthetic latent classes and compare edit distance, shared affixes, frequency, line position, section, and local-copy baselines. Run the same pipeline on synthetic meaningful encodings and structured nonsemantic copy/mutate generators. Split by pages and generator instances, not random occurrences. Only then ask whether a small set of hypothesized Voynich relations survives independent textual/image evidence and an executable decoding rule. Near neighbours alone can be morphology, copying, section, scribal style, or codebook artifacts.

One possible evaluation ladder is:

1. **Calibration:** known synthetic generator with ground-truth latent roles, including a copy/mutate generator that creates plausible visual neighborhoods without the same roles. Train on matched sample sizes and report retrieval or pairwise role accuracy on unseen generator instances.
2. **Nuisance control:** predict edit distance, prefix/suffix overlap, frequency, line position and folio section from each representation. A target relation should survive matching or residualizing these variables; residualization itself must be fitted on training material only.
3. **Functional test:** for a fixed task model, measure whether two candidate neighbor edits produce similar *held-out causal output profiles*, not just similar vectors. Compare raw cosine, centered cosine, regularized Fisher/Jacobian geometry, and a simple spelling/co-occurrence baseline with the same selection budget.
4. **Historical claim gate:** on untouched physical folios, a small frozen relation set must improve an executable decipherment/generation rule against structured nulls and independent anchors. The relation labels and exceptions must be frozen before that test. Failure here leaves only a structural or model-internal finding.

The ordering matters because synthetic role accuracy can be achieved through a known channel family and still fail transfer to a different manuscript generator. Conversely, manuscript context prediction can improve without recovering a historically intended meaning. The independent anchor must be specified in advance—e.g. a codicologically justified repeated label with an independently identified referent, not a plant resemblance chosen after viewing neighbors.

For the immediate PATH-0003 route study, arbitrary names/keys/answers deliberately have no shared semantic categories. Pairwise cosine across them would be hard to interpret. We therefore prioritize **attention-window causal cuts** on a new composed-query task and defer vocabulary mapping. The cut effects will later provide a well-defined functional target against which similarity metrics can be tested. This is a decision about experimental order, not a finding that cosine is useless.
